Exploring the Differential Impact of the Repeal of the ACA Individual Mandate by Health Status

Health insurance mandates are designed to mitigate adverse selection by encouraging enrollment among relatively healthy individuals. This paper examines how the 2019 repeal of the Affordable Care Act (ACA) individual mandate penalty affected insurance participation, Marketplace premiums, out-of-pocket premium spending, and consumer surplus by health status. Using a two-way fixed effects design, I find that the repeal increased the probability of becoming newly uninsured by 0.56 percentage points (27 percent relative to the pre-reform mean), with nearly all of the increase driven by relatively healthy consumers. Benchmark Marketplace premiums increased by approximately $739, while average out-of-pocket premium spending decreased by $284.60 as ACA premium subsidies offset much of the premium increase. A consumer surplus analysis shows that, without premium subsidies, relatively sick consumers bear most of the welfare losses from adverse selection. Accounting for subsidies substantially mitigates these losses, demonstrating that premium subsidies alter the distributional consequences of adverse selection.

Does the Tobacco 21 Minimum Legal Sales Age Policy Improve Respiratory Health? Evidence from the United States

With M.O. Emmanuel, G. Aipoh, V.J. Dickson, and E.N. Collins

Revise and Resubmit, Social Science & Medicine

Tobacco control policies are widely evaluated based on their effects on smoking behavior, but less is known about their short-term health effects. This study examines whether Tobacco 21 minimum legal sales age (MLSA) laws reduce smoking and asthma prevalence among individuals aged 18–20. Using Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) data from 2011–2019 and variation in the timing of policy adoption across states, we find that Tobacco 21 laws significantly reduce smoking but do not lead to a statistically significant decline in asthma prevalence in the short run. Subgroup analyses suggest larger reductions in asthma prevalence among Black young adults and 19-year-olds, although these estimates are statistically insignificant. These findings suggest that reductions in smoking following Tobacco 21 laws may not immediately translate into measurable improvements in respiratory health and highlight the importance of considering the timing of health effects when evaluating tobacco control policies.

Spillover Effects of Prescription Drug Insurance on Informal Caregiving: Evidence from Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) Medicare Recipients

Work in Progress

I study whether the benefits of prescription drug insurance extend beyond the insured individual by reducing the need for informal care. Using Health and Retirement Study data and a difference-in-differences estimator that accounts for heterogeneous treatment effects, I examine how improved access to prescription medications affects the functional health of SSDI Medicare beneficiaries and the need for care from family members.

The findings suggest that improved access to prescription medications enhances functional health and reduces the need for informal care, indicating that evaluations based only on direct benefits to insured individuals may understate the broader welfare gains of health insurance.
